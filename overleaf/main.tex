% ABSTRACT (~200 words)

\begin{abstract}
Body Area Networks (BANs) require secure intra-body communication, yet sensor nodes are too resource-constrained for conventional public-key cryptography, and pre-shared key schemes conflict with plug-and-play clinical workflows. This paper introduces \textit{PhysioKey}, a TinyML-based key agreement framework that derives symmetric session keys from physiological signals without pre-shared secrets or trusted third parties. A lightweight 1D convolutional neural network (6,320 parameters, INT8-quantized, 31.2~KB flash) extracts 32-dimensional embeddings from ECG signal windows on ARM Cortex-M4 class devices. Embeddings are quantized via Gray coding and reconciled through a fuzzy commitment scheme with BCH error-correcting codes. Simulation on 500 patients from the PhysioNet PTB-XL dataset, using Lead~I versus Lead~II as a conservative multi-sensor proxy, yields intra-body cosine similarity of $0.703$ versus inter-body $0.241$ (ROC AUC $= 0.943$, EER $= 13.3\%$). A fundamental trade-off governs key agreement: BCH(63,16,23) achieves 73.2\% success with a 16-bit effective key, while BCH(63,7,31) reaches 93.6\% success but yields only 7 effective key bits. Total latency is 3.6~ms and energy is 0.345~mJ per agreement---roughly $20\times$ below ECC-256 ECDH. Limitations including modest effective key lengths and the use of a proxy dataset are discussed candidly.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Body area network, physiological key agreement, TinyML, edge AI, ECG, PPG, fuzzy commitment, healthcare IoT security.
\end{IEEEkeywords}}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{B}{ody} Area Networks (BANs) enable continuous physiological monitoring through miniaturized sensors placed on or within the human body~\cite{movassaghi2014wireless, cao2009enabling}. The wearable medical device market has grown rapidly in recent years, driven by remote patient monitoring, chronic disease management, and post-surgical care~\cite{al2017body}. In a typical deployment, heterogeneous sensor nodes---electrocardiogram (ECG) electrodes, photoplethysmogram (PPG) sensors, accelerometers, temperature probes---communicate wirelessly with a body-worn coordinator, which relays data to clinical systems~\cite{chen2011body}.

Securing intra-BAN communication is both critical and difficult. Physiological data falls under strict regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR~\cite{li2010data}. Interception or manipulation of BAN traffic could compromise patient privacy, falsify clinical readings, or disrupt therapeutic devices like insulin pumps~\cite{rushanan2014sok}. Yet BAN sensor nodes operate under severe constraints: ARM Cortex-M class processors at 64--168~MHz, 256--512~KB flash, 64--128~KB SRAM, powered by coin-cell batteries of 100--300~mAh~\cite{zhang2017security}. Traditional Public Key Infrastructure is impractical given the computational burden of asymmetric operations and certificate overhead~\cite{kompara2018survey}.

Existing BAN key establishment approaches fall into three categories, each with notable shortcomings. \textit{Pre-shared key} schemes require manual configuration or a trusted authority, creating deployment friction incompatible with dynamic clinical environments~\cite{eschenauer2002random}. \textit{Lightweight public-key} approaches based on Elliptic Curve Cryptography reduce cost relative to RSA but still demand energy-intensive point multiplications on constrained hardware~\cite{chan2003random}. \textit{Physiological signal correlation} methods exploit the shared biological origin of signals measured at different body sites to derive common secrets~\cite{poon2006novel, venkatasubramanian2010pska, rostami2013heart}. These are appealing conceptually, but existing implementations rely on hand-crafted features---inter-pulse intervals, peak amplitudes---that exhibit limited discriminative power, high sensitivity to sensor placement, and no adaptation to individual patients~\cite{xu2017walkie}.

The gap at the intersection of these approaches is clear: no existing scheme provides an adaptive, learning-based key agreement mechanism that fits within BAN resource constraints while eliminating pre-shared secrets. TinyML---machine learning inference on microcontrollers with milliwatt power budgets---offers a path forward~\cite{banbury2021mlperf, warden2019tinyml}. By deploying learned feature extractors on sensor nodes, patient-specific physiological patterns can be captured that are simultaneously correlated between co-located sensors and difficult for external adversaries to predict.

This paper introduces \textit{PhysioKey}, an Edge-AI-driven physiological key agreement framework. The core idea is that a lightweight 1D-CNN, quantized to INT8 and occupying 31.2~KB flash, can extract embedding vectors from physiological signal windows that reflect the body's shared physiological state while remaining robust to sensor noise and placement variation. These embeddings are reconciled through a fuzzy commitment scheme to produce symmetric session keys. The principal contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A TinyML-based physiological key agreement framework that uses a learned 1D-CNN feature extractor to derive correlated embeddings from ECG signals on Cortex-M4 class devices, enabling plug-and-play enrollment without pre-shared secrets.

    \item A formal threat model for intra-BAN communication covering passive eavesdropping, active replay, impersonation, and model inversion attacks.

    \item An honest characterization of the trade-off between key agreement success rate and effective key length, with entropy analysis that distinguishes the entropy of the quantized representation from the entropy of the source signal.

    \item A simulation-based evaluation on 500 patients from PTB-XL, demonstrating EER~$= 13.3\%$, ROC~AUC~$= 0.943$, and key agreement success rates ranging from 33.8\% (24 effective key bits) to 93.6\% (7 effective key bits) for the 1-bit configuration, with 3.6~ms latency and 0.345~mJ energy per agreement.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work. Section~\ref{sec:system_model} formalizes system and threat models. Section~\ref{sec:framework} details the PhysioKey framework. Section~\ref{sec:security} presents the security analysis. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports evaluation results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section surveys five related areas and identifies the gap motivating PhysioKey.

\subsection{Key Distribution in Wireless Sensor Networks}
\label{subsec:key_dist}

Early key management schemes for wireless sensor networks established approaches that influenced BAN security. Eschenauer and Gligor~\cite{eschenauer2002random} proposed probabilistic key pre-distribution where each node is preloaded with a random key subset from a global pool. While this eliminates online key exchange, it provides only probabilistic connectivity and requires a trusted authority. Chan \etal~\cite{chan2003random} extended this with $q$-composite pre-distribution, trading network scalability for improved capture resilience.

Zhu \etal~\cite{zhu2006leap} introduced LEAP+, which establishes four key types---individual, pairwise, cluster, and group---through pre-deployment material and localized post-deployment establishment. LEAP+ supports efficient revocation but depends on a static key hierarchy unsuited to BANs, where sensors are frequently repositioned during clinical workflows.

The fundamental limitation of all pre-distribution approaches is their assumption of deployment-time trust. In clinical BAN scenarios, a nurse may attach several sensors within minutes, and these must immediately establish secure channels without manual key entry or centralized coordination.

\subsection{Physiological Signal-Based Security}
\label{subsec:physio_security}

Exploiting the human body as a source of shared randomness for BAN key agreement was pioneered by Poon \etal~\cite{poon2006novel}, who showed that inter-pulse intervals (IPIs) measured simultaneously at different body locations exhibit sufficient correlation to serve as a common secret. Their scheme quantizes IPIs and uses error-correcting codes for reconciliation. While foundational, the reliance on a single hand-crafted feature makes it vulnerable to external estimation and degrades in patients with arrhythmias.

Venkatasubramanian \etal~\cite{venkatasubramanian2010pska} proposed PSKA, extending IPI-based approaches with multiple features including peak amplitude and frequency-domain characteristics. PSKA reduces the number of signal windows needed for agreement but still relies on predetermined feature functions that do not adapt to individual physiology.

Rostami \etal~\cite{rostami2013heart} presented Heart-to-Heart (H2H), using ECG features for key agreement between an implantable device and an external programmer. H2H provides formal guarantees under the random oracle model but targets the implant-programmer scenario specifically, not the multi-sensor BAN setting.

Xu \etal~\cite{xu2017walkie} proposed Walkie-Talkie, using gait patterns from accelerometers for key agreement. This is inapplicable to bedridden patients or stationary monitoring common in intensive care.

\subsection{Biometric Key Agreement Mechanisms}
\label{subsec:biometric}

Fuzzy extractors, introduced by Dodis \etal~\cite{dodis2008fuzzy}, provide the theoretical foundation for deriving keys from noisy biometric data. A fuzzy extractor has two procedures: $\mathsf{Gen}(w) \rightarrow (R, P)$, producing a random string $R$ and helper string $P$; and $\mathsf{Rep}(w', P) \rightarrow R$, recovering $R$ from a nearby measurement $w'$ using $P$, provided $d(w, w') \leq t$. Security requires that $R$ remain computationally indistinguishable from random even given $P$.

Juels and Wattenberg~\cite{juels1999fuzzy} proposed the fuzzy commitment scheme, combining error-correcting codes with hashing to bind a secret to a noisy measurement. The commit phase computes $c = \mathsf{ECC}(K) \oplus w$ and $h = \mathcal{H}(K)$; the decommit phase recovers $K$ from $c \oplus w'$ by decoding, provided $d(w, w') \leq t_{\text{ECC}}$.

These mechanisms are central to PhysioKey's key derivation, enabling two sensors measuring correlated but non-identical signals to agree on the same key without revealing the underlying features.

\subsection{Edge AI and TinyML for IoT Security}
\label{subsec:tinyml}

TinyML enables machine learning on microcontrollers with milliwatt power budgets and kilobyte memory footprints~\cite{warden2019tinyml, banbury2021mlperf}. TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers and similar frameworks support INT8 quantized inference on ARM Cortex-M processors, achieving millisecond-scale inference for compact models~\cite{david2021tensorflow}.

In security, TinyML has been applied to IoT anomaly detection~\cite{antonini2023tiny}, hardware fingerprinting for device authentication~\cite{chatterjee2019rf}, and intrusion detection on edge devices~\cite{alhanahnah2023tinyml}. However, the application of TinyML to \textit{key agreement} remains largely unexplored. Existing TinyML security work treats models as classifiers or anomaly detectors, not as feature extractors whose outputs drive cryptographic key derivation.

Sriram and Rajan~\cite{sriram2022tinyml} showed that 1D-CNNs quantized to INT8 can classify ECG on Cortex-M4 devices in under 15~ms, suggesting similar architectures could serve feature extraction in key agreement. Lin \etal~\cite{lin2020mcunet} proposed MCUNet for neural architecture search within MCU constraints, establishing that meaningful deep learning fits within the 256~KB flash / 64~KB SRAM envelope of BAN nodes.

\section{System Model and Threat Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{subsec:architecture}

The target deployment is a BAN comprising $N$ sensor nodes $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N\}$ attached to a patient's body, communicating wirelessly with a body-worn coordinator $\mathcal{C}$. Each node is modeled as an ARM Cortex-M4 class device: 168~MHz clock, 512~KB flash, 128~KB SRAM, BLE 5.0 radio, 225~mAh coin-cell battery. The coordinator aggregates data and relays it to a gateway or cloud backend.

The BAN topology is a single-hop star centered on $\mathcal{C}$, with pairwise secure channels required between any sensor and the coordinator, as well as between specific sensor pairs for collaborative sensing (\eg, fusing ECG and PPG for blood pressure estimation). Sensors are positioned at clinically relevant locations: ECG electrodes at the chest (Lead~II configuration), PPG sensors at the wrist or fingertip, and auxiliary sensors elsewhere.

\subsection{Signal Model}
\label{subsec:signal_model}

Let $x_i(t) \in \mathbb{R}$ denote the physiological signal sampled by sensor $s_i$ at time $t$. For ECG sensors, $x_i(t)$ represents the electrical potential measured at chest electrodes; for PPG sensors, $x_j(t)$ represents infrared light absorption at a peripheral site. Both are sampled at $f_s = 256$~Hz, satisfying the Nyquist criterion for relevant frequency bands (ECG: 0.5--40~Hz; PPG: 0.5--8~Hz)~\cite{clifford2006advanced}.

A signal window $\mathbf{x}_i^{(k)} = [x_i(kW), x_i(kW+1), \ldots, x_i(kW+L-1)]^T \in \mathbb{R}^L$ is a segment of $L = 256$ samples (1~second at 256~Hz) starting at epoch $k$. The feature extractor $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^L \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ maps a window to a $d$-dimensional embedding $\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i^{(k)}) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $d = 32$.

The property exploited by PhysioKey is that signals from the same body exhibit high cross-correlation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:correlation}
\rho(\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)}, \mathbf{z}_j^{(k)}) = \frac{\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)} \cdot \mathbf{z}_j^{(k)}}{\|\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{z}_j^{(k)}\|} \geq \tau, \quad \forall s_i, s_j \in \mathcal{S}
\end{equation}
while signals from different bodies satisfy $\rho(\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)}, \mathbf{z}_{j'}^{(k)}) < \tau$ with high probability.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{subsec:threat_model}

The adversary $\mathcal{A}$ is defined with the following capabilities and limitations, consistent with the Dolev-Yao model adapted for BANs~\cite{dolev1983security}:

\textbf{Adversary capabilities.} $\mathcal{A}$ can: (C1)~observe all wireless transmissions, including ciphertexts and metadata; (C2)~inject arbitrary packets; (C3)~replay previously captured messages; (C4)~obtain the model $f_\theta$ (white-box access); (C5)~perform unlimited offline computation.

\textbf{Adversary limitations.} $\mathcal{A}$ cannot: (L1)~physically attach sensors to the patient or measure their physiological signals in real time; (L2)~tamper with hardware or firmware of legitimately deployed nodes; (L3)~be co-located on the same body.

\textbf{Justification.} Limitation L1 is the fundamental physical assumption: the adversary lacks physical access to the patient during key agreement. This is reasonable in clinical settings where sensor attachment is performed by authorized staff. L2 assumes standard tamper resistance, and L3 follows from L1.

\textbf{Attack types addressed:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Eavesdropping}: $\mathcal{A}$ observes all channel traffic and attempts to derive $K$.
    \item \textit{Replay}: $\mathcal{A}$ replays a captured transcript to establish a session with stale keys.
    \item \textit{Impersonation}: $\mathcal{A}$ introduces a rogue sensor and attempts to pass the protocol.
    \item \textit{Model inversion}: $\mathcal{A}$, possessing $f_\theta$, attempts to infer raw signals from protocol messages to forge embeddings.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Security goal.} The protocol must ensure:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:security_goal}
\Pr[K_{\mathcal{A}} = K_{\text{legit}}] \leq 2^{-\lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the security parameter. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:security}, the effective security depends on the entropy of the agreed bits, which is modest (7--24 bits for the 1-bit configuration). SHA-256 key derivation provides computational stretching but does not create information-theoretic security beyond the source entropy.

\section{Proposed Framework: PhysioKey}
\label{sec:framework}

This section details the PhysioKey framework: the system pipeline, TinyML model, key derivation, enrollment protocol, and key refresh mechanism.

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{subsec:overview}

PhysioKey operates in five stages, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Signal Capture}: Sensors $s_i$ and $s_j$ synchronously capture windows $\mathbf{x}_i^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{x}_j^{(k)}$ during epoch $k$.
    \item \textbf{TinyML Feature Extraction}: Each sensor applies the learned extractor $f_\theta$ to produce embeddings $\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{z}_j^{(k)}$.
    \item \textbf{Quantization}: Embeddings are quantized to binary strings $\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)}, \mathbf{b}_j^{(k)} \in \{0,1\}^n$.
    \item \textbf{Fuzzy Commitment}: The initiator $s_i$ computes a commitment using BCH codes and transmits it to $s_j$.
    \item \textbf{Key Agreement}: The responder $s_j$ uses the commitment and its own quantized embedding to recover the session key $K$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{TinyML Feature Extraction Model}
\label{subsec:tinyml_model}

The feature extractor $f_\theta$ is a 1D-CNN designed to satisfy three objectives: (O1)~maximize mutual information $I(\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)}; \mathbf{z}_j^{(k)})$ between embeddings from co-located sensors on the same body; (O2)~minimize mutual information $I(\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)}; \mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{A}})$ between legitimate embeddings and signals observable by the adversary; (O3)~fit the MCU resource envelope ($\leq$~50~KB flash, $\leq$~20~KB SRAM, $\leq$~15~ms inference).

\textbf{Architecture.} The model consists of three convolutional blocks followed by global average pooling and two fully connected layers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input}: $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 1}$ (1-second window)
    \item \textbf{Conv1D-1}: 8 filters, kernel size 5, stride 2, BN, ReLU $\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 8}$
    \item \textbf{Conv1D-2}: 16 filters, kernel size 3, stride 2, BN, ReLU $\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 16}$
    \item \textbf{Conv1D-3}: 32 filters, kernel size 3, stride 2, BN, ReLU $\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{32 \times 32}$
    \item \textbf{Global Average Pooling}: $\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{32}$
    \item \textbf{Dense-1}: 64 units, ReLU $\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{64}$
    \item \textbf{Dense-2}: 32 units, linear $\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{32}$ (embedding $\mathbf{z}$)
\end{enumerate}

Under INT8 quantization, each parameter occupies 1~byte, giving a model size of approximately 6.2~KB for weights. With the TFLM runtime, quantization metadata, and tensor arena, the total flash footprint is 31.2~KB.

\textbf{Training objective.} The model is trained offline on synchronized multi-sensor physiological recordings. The training loss combines contrastive, decorrelation, and alignment terms:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{-\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{P}} \log \frac{e^{\text{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_j)/\tau_T}}{\sum_{(i,n) \in \mathcal{N}} e^{\text{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_n)/\tau_T}}}_{\text{Contrastive}} + \lambda \underbrace{\|\mathbf{C}_z - \mathbf{I}\|_F^2}_{\text{Decorrelation}} + \mu \underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{P}} \|\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_j\|_2^2}_{\text{Alignment}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ denotes positive pairs (same body, same epoch), $\mathcal{N}$ denotes negative pairs (different bodies or temporally separated epochs), $\text{sim}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is cosine similarity, $\tau_T$ is a temperature parameter, $\mathbf{C}_z$ is the feature correlation matrix, $\lambda$ controls decorrelation strength, and $\mu$ weights the alignment loss. The alignment term directly penalizes Euclidean distance between positive-pair embeddings, encouraging the model to produce similar representations for co-located sensors measuring the same physiological event. The decorrelation term promotes statistical independence between embedding dimensions. In the experiments reported here, $\tau_T = 0.05$, $\lambda = 0.05$, and $\mu = 0.5$.

\textbf{Quantization.} Post-training quantization converts weights and activations from FP32 to INT8 using the TensorFlow Lite converter with representative calibration data. This reduces model size by $4\times$ and enables fixed-point arithmetic on the Cortex-M4 integer pipeline.

\subsection{Key Derivation via Fuzzy Commitment}
\label{subsec:key_derivation}

Given the quantized embedding $\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)} \in \{0,1\}^n$ from sensor $s_i$, key derivation proceeds as follows.

\textbf{Step 1: Embedding quantization.} The continuous embedding $\mathbf{z}_i^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ is quantized to a binary string using multi-level Gray coding:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multilevel_quant}
Q_{\text{multi}}(\mathbf{z}) = \bigoplus_{l=1}^{d} \text{Gray}_q\!\left(\left\lfloor \frac{z_l - z_l^{\min}}{z_l^{\max} - z_l^{\min}} \cdot (2^q - 1) \right\rfloor\right)
\end{equation}
where $\text{Gray}_q(\cdot)$ maps a $q$-bit integer to its Gray code representation. Two configurations are evaluated: (i)~$q = 1$ yielding $\mathbf{b} \in \{0,1\}^{32}$ and (ii)~$q = 2$ yielding $\mathbf{b} \in \{0,1\}^{64}$.

\textbf{Step 2: Zero-padding and BCH commitment.} Because standard BCH codes operate on block lengths of the form $2^m - 1$, the raw quantized bits must be zero-padded to the nearest valid block length. For $q = 1$ (32 raw bits), the bits are padded to length 63 and processed with BCH(63,$k_c$,$d_{\min}$) codes. For $q = 2$ (64 raw bits), the bits are padded to length 127 and processed with BCH(127,$k_c$,$d_{\min}$) codes. The initiator $s_i$ generates a random key $K$ and computes:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:commitment}
c &= \text{BCH}_{(n_c, k_c, t)}\text{-Encode}(K) \oplus \text{pad}(\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)}) \\
h &= \text{SHA-256}(K \| \text{nonce})
\end{align}
where $k_c$ is the dimension of the BCH code (and thus the effective key length in bits), and $\text{nonce}$ is a fresh random value. The tuple $(c, h, \text{nonce})$ is transmitted to $s_j$.

\textbf{Step 3: Key recovery.} The responder $s_j$ computes:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:recovery}
\hat{c} &= c \oplus \text{pad}(\mathbf{b}_j^{(k)}) \\
\hat{K} &= \text{BCH-Decode}(\hat{c})
\end{align}
If $d_H(\text{pad}(\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)}), \text{pad}(\mathbf{b}_j^{(k)})) \leq t$, the decoder recovers $K$. Correctness is verified by checking $h \stackrel{?}{=} \text{SHA-256}(\hat{K} \| \text{nonce})$.

\textbf{Trade-off between success rate and key length.} The choice of BCH parameters governs a fundamental trade-off. Higher error-correction capability $t$ increases the success rate but reduces $k_c$---the number of effective key bits. For instance, with the 1-bit configuration and BCH(63,16,23), $t = 11$ corrects up to 11 of the 63 padded bits and yields 16-bit effective keys with 73.2\% success. With BCH(63,7,31) and $t = 15$, success rises to 93.6\% but the effective key is only 7 bits. This trade-off is characterized fully in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Plug-and-Play Enrollment Protocol}
\label{subsec:enrollment}

PhysioKey supports the addition of new sensors without prior key material. When a new sensor $s_{\text{new}}$ is attached, enrollment proceeds as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:enrollment}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{PhysioKey Plug-and-Play Enrollment}
\label{alg:enrollment}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE New sensor $s_{\text{new}}$, existing sensor $s_j$, TinyML model $f_\theta$
\ENSURE Shared session key $K$ or rejection
\STATE $s_{\text{new}}$ captures signal window $\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}^{(k)}$
\STATE $s_{\text{new}}$ computes $\mathbf{z}_{\text{new}}^{(k)} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}^{(k)})$
\STATE $s_j$ captures $\mathbf{x}_j^{(k)}$ during the same epoch $k$
\STATE $s_j$ computes $\mathbf{z}_j^{(k)} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_j^{(k)})$
\STATE Both sensors quantize: $\mathbf{b}_{\text{new}}^{(k)}, \mathbf{b}_j^{(k)}$
\STATE $s_j$ initiates fuzzy commitment with $s_{\text{new}}$
\IF{$\text{SHA-256}(\hat{K} \| \text{nonce}) = h$}
    \STATE \textbf{Accept}: $s_{\text{new}}$ is on the same body; key $K$ established
\ELSE
    \STATE \textbf{Reject}: $s_{\text{new}}$ is not on the same body
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

No pre-shared secrets, certificates, or manual pairing are required. The only prerequisite is that $s_{\text{new}}$ carries the same model $f_\theta$, which is public (consistent with Kerckhoffs' principle) and distributed via firmware updates.

\textbf{Group-wise key establishment.} For $N$ sensors, the coordinator $\mathcal{C}$ establishes pairwise keys $K_{i,\mathcal{C}}$ with each sensor $s_i$, then distributes a group key $K_G$ encrypted under each pairwise key: $E_{K_{i,\mathcal{C}}}(K_G)$. This requires $N$ pairwise agreements rather than $\binom{N}{2}$.

\subsection{Key Refresh Mechanism}
\label{subsec:key_refresh}

To prevent replay attacks and provide key independence across epochs, PhysioKey refreshes session keys periodically. Each agreement is bound to a temporal epoch:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:epoch}
k = \left\lfloor \frac{t - t_0}{\Delta} \right\rfloor
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ is a synchronized reference time and $\Delta$ is the epoch duration (default: 60~seconds). Sensors maintain loose time synchronization via BLE beacon timestamps, with $\pm 50$~ms tolerance (within the 1-second window).

At each epoch, sensors capture fresh windows and derive new keys. Previous key material is erased from SRAM. This provides:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Replay resistance}: A replayed commitment from epoch $k'$ fails because the responder's embedding $\mathbf{z}_j^{(k)}$ differs from $\mathbf{z}_j^{(k')}$ due to physiological evolution.
    \item \textit{Key independence}: Each key is derived from a distinct signal window. Since no long-term keying material exists---$f_\theta$ is public and no master secret is stored---compromise of session key $K^{(k)}$ does not reveal $K^{(k')}$ for $k' \neq k$. This property is better described as key independence rather than forward secrecy in the traditional sense, since there is no long-term secret whose compromise would retroactively threaten past sessions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Entropy Analysis}
\label{subsec:entropy}

The security of PhysioKey depends on the entropy of the derived key, which is determined by the entropy of the quantized embedding $\mathbf{b}^{(k)}$.

\textbf{Quantized representation entropy vs.\ source entropy.} An important distinction must be drawn. The quantization boundaries in PhysioKey are set using percentiles of the training distribution (\eg, median for 1-bit, quartiles for 2-bit). By construction, percentile-based boundaries produce approximately uniform bin occupancy, so the per-dimension entropy of the \textit{quantized representation} approaches the theoretical maximum of $q$ bits. However, this does not imply that the underlying source signal carries $q$ bits of entropy per dimension---the uniform bin occupancy is an artifact of the quantization design, not a property of the signal itself. What matters for security is whether an adversary who cannot measure the body's signals can predict the quantized output; the relevant quantity is the conditional entropy $H(\mathbf{b}^{(k)} | \mathcal{A}\text{'s observations})$, which is discussed below.

\begin{theorem}[Entropy of PhysioKey Quantized Embeddings]
\label{thm:entropy}
Let $\mathbf{z}^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ be the embedding produced by $f_\theta$. Under percentile-based $q$-bit quantization with decorrelation regularization driving inter-dimension correlation toward zero, the entropy of the quantized string $\mathbf{b}^{(k)} \in \{0,1\}^{dq}$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quant_entropy}
H(\mathbf{b}^{(k)}) \leq d \cdot q \text{ bits (with equality when dimensions are independent)}
\end{equation}
For $q = 1$: $H(\mathbf{b}^{(k)}) \leq 32$ bits. For $q = 2$: $H(\mathbf{b}^{(k)}) \leq 64$ bits.
\end{theorem}

The decorrelation loss term encourages independence between dimensions, and empirical analysis on PTB-XL shows near-maximum per-dimension entropy. But these are upper bounds on the entropy of the representation; the actual min-entropy available for key derivation is lower once BCH redundancy is accounted for.

\textbf{Effective key entropy after fuzzy commitment.} The fuzzy commitment leaks information through the helper data $c$. Following Dodis \etal~\cite{dodis2008fuzzy}, the entropy loss is bounded by the BCH code redundancy $n_c - k_c$. The effective key entropy is therefore $k_c$ bits---the message dimension of the BCH code, which equals the length of the random key $K$. Concretely:

\begin{itemize}
    \item BCH(63,24,15) with $t=7$: effective key entropy $= 24$ bits
    \item BCH(63,16,23) with $t=11$: effective key entropy $= 16$ bits
    \item BCH(63,7,31) with $t=15$: effective key entropy $= 7$ bits
\end{itemize}

These are modest values. The SHA-256 key derivation $K_{\text{session}} = \text{SHA-256}(K \| \text{nonce} \| \text{context})$ stretches the output to 256 bits, providing \textit{computational} security under the random oracle model. However, the information-theoretic security remains bounded by the effective key bits. An adversary with unlimited computation could brute-force a 7-bit key in $2^7 = 128$ trials. The practical security depends on rate-limiting key agreement attempts and the physical limitation that an adversary cannot repeatedly trigger the protocol.

\textbf{Conditional entropy against an external adversary.} Since $\mathcal{A}$ cannot measure the body's physiological signals (limitation L1), the adversary's best strategy is random guessing of the padded bit string. For the 1-bit configuration with BCH(63,7,31) and $t = 15$:
\begin{equation}
\Pr[d_H(\text{pad}(\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)}), \mathbf{b}_{\mathcal{A}}) \leq t] = \sum_{j=0}^{15} \binom{63}{j} \cdot 2^{-63}
\end{equation}

Computing this sum: $\sum_{j=0}^{15}\binom{63}{j} \approx 2^{62 \cdot H_2(15/63)} \approx 2^{62 \times 0.817} \approx 2^{50.7}$, so the probability is approximately $2^{50.7 - 63} = 2^{-12.3}$. This is not negligible in the cryptographic sense. The security against random guessing for the BCH(63,7,31) configuration is roughly equivalent to 12 bits of security---adequate for a session key refreshed every 60 seconds but far below standard cryptographic thresholds. Stronger BCH codes (\eg, BCH(63,24,15) with $t = 7$) yield lower guessing probability but also lower success rates (33.8\%).

\subsection{Attack Resistance}
\label{subsec:attack_resistance}

\textbf{Eavesdropping resistance.} The adversary observes $(c, h, \text{nonce})$. The commitment $c = \text{BCH-Encode}(K) \oplus \text{pad}(\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)})$ is computationally indistinguishable from random without $\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)}$, since $K$ is uniformly random. The hash $h = \text{SHA-256}(K \| \text{nonce})$ reveals no information about $K$ under the random oracle model.

\textbf{Replay resistance.} Temporal drift analysis on PTB-XL shows that non-adjacent windows from the same patient produce a mean Hamming distance of $30.2$ bits on 64-bit embeddings, with 99.2\% of replayed vectors exceeding BCH correction capability:
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\text{replay succeeds}] \leq 0.008
\end{equation}
The temporal variability of physiological signals makes replayed commitments from previous epochs ineffective.

\textbf{Impersonation resistance.} A rogue sensor not on the patient produces embeddings with mean cosine similarity of $0.241$ to legitimate sensors (versus $0.703$ for intra-body pairs). The resulting Hamming distances far exceed BCH correction capacity, yielding impersonation probability bounded by FAR. At the EER threshold ($\tau = 0.497$), $\text{FAR} = 13.3\%$. This FAR is a significant security limitation that must be acknowledged: roughly 1 in 8 impersonation attempts by an adversary with a random physiological signal would produce embeddings close enough to fall within the acceptance region based on cosine similarity alone. However, the impersonator must \textit{also} produce quantized bits within Hamming distance $t$ of the legitimate bits, and the BCH decoding must succeed, which substantially reduces the effective impersonation rate. The combined false acceptance through the full fuzzy commitment pipeline is lower than the cosine-similarity FAR, though it remains a concern for high-security applications.

\textbf{Model inversion resistance.} Even with white-box access to $f_\theta$: (1)~$f_\theta$ is many-to-one ($\mathbb{R}^{256} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{32}$), making inversion infeasible; (2)~recovering $\mathbf{b}_i^{(k)}$ from $c$ requires knowing $K$, creating a circular dependency.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This section reports simulation-based evaluation of PhysioKey using the PhysioNet PTB-XL dataset and analytical resource estimates for Cortex-M4 hardware.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:datasets}

The evaluation uses the PTB-XL database~\cite{goldberger2000physiobank, wagner2020ptbxl}, which contains 21,837 12-lead ECG recordings from 18,885 patients sampled at 500~Hz. Lead~I and Lead~II are extracted as proxies for two different sensor modalities on the same body. These leads share a common cardiovascular origin but exhibit distinct waveform morphologies, providing a conservative model of ECG-PPG pairing where co-located sensors would likely show higher correlation.

From 500 patients, 200 are used for training and 100 for testing (the remaining 200 are held out). This yields 2,000 training window pairs and 1,000 test window pairs. It should be noted that this is a single train/test split without cross-validation; results may vary across different splits.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Raw signals are: (1)~resampled from 500~Hz to 256~Hz; (2)~bandpass filtered at 0.5--40~Hz with a fourth-order Butterworth filter; (3)~normalized to zero mean and unit variance per recording; (4)~segmented into non-overlapping 1-second windows ($L = 256$). Low-quality windows (standard deviation $< 0.1$) are discarded.

\subsection{Simulation Setup}
\label{subsec:sim_setup}

The simulation uses Python 3.12 with PyTorch 2.5.1 (CPU). Training runs for 150 epochs with the Adam optimizer (learning rate $5 \times 10^{-4}$, batch size 128), cosine annealing schedule, and the combined loss from Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}: NT-Xent contrastive ($\tau_T = 0.05$), decorrelation ($\lambda = 0.05$), and alignment ($\mu = 0.5$). Gray code quantization and BCH error correction are implemented in Python. Hardware estimates are analytical, based on ARM Cortex-M4 (STM32F407) specifications: 168~MHz, 1.25~DMIPS/MHz, 0.287~mW/MHz active power, with CMSIS-NN kernels~\cite{lai2018cmsis}.

\textbf{Inference breakdown.} The 2.1~ms CNN inference is derived from approximately 82,944 MACs at the Cortex-M4 throughput of roughly 40~MMAC/s with CMSIS-NN kernels~\cite{lai2018cmsis}. Batch normalization folds into convolution weights at inference time. The model requires 6,320 parameters (6.2~KB at INT8), with total flash footprint of 31.2~KB including the TFLM runtime.

\textbf{Energy.} Total energy per key agreement across both sensors is $2 \times 0.173 = 0.345$~mJ, using 48.2~mW active power. This is roughly $20\times$ lower than ECC-256 ECDH (6.84~mJ) and $508\times$ lower than RSA-2048 (87.7~mJ). A 225~mAh coin-cell at 3.0~V could support approximately 7~million PhysioKey agreements before depletion.

\textbf{Memory.} PhysioKey requires 37.6~KB flash (7.3\% of 512~KB) and 10.4~KB SRAM (8.1\% of 128~KB), leaving room for the BLE stack, application firmware, and data buffers.

% VII. DISCUSSION
% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Effective key length.} The most prominent limitation is that high success rates require aggressive error correction, which reduces the effective key to as few as 7 bits. A 7-bit key offers minimal information-theoretic security. Even the 16-bit key from BCH(63,16,23) is well below standard cryptographic key lengths. SHA-256 key derivation provides computational hardness but cannot substitute for entropy that is not there. Two paths toward improvement exist: (1)~reducing BDR through co-located ECG-PPG sensors rather than the Lead~I/II proxy, and (2)~multi-signal fusion to increase embedding dimensionality and thus the total bit budget. Both are left to future hardware-based experiments.

\textbf{FAR as a security limitation.} The 8.6\% FAR at $\tau = 0.55$ (or 12.9\% at the balanced operating point $\tau = 0.50$) is not just a performance metric---it represents the probability that an adversary's sensor, measuring an unrelated physiological signal, produces embeddings similar enough to a legitimate sensor to potentially succeed in key agreement. While the full fuzzy commitment pipeline adds additional filtering (the adversary's quantized bits must also fall within Hamming distance $t$), the FAR remains a meaningful security concern. In high-security applications, PhysioKey should be combined with other authentication mechanisms.

\textbf{Conservative evaluation proxy.} The use of Lead~I versus Lead~II from 12-lead ECG recordings is a conservative stand-in for the intended ECG-PPG pairing scenario. These leads capture different electrical vectors of the heart with distinct morphologies. True ECG-PPG pairing from co-located sensors shares local hemodynamic characteristics and is expected to yield lower BDR, improving both success rates and effective key lengths at a given BCH configuration. Validating this requires hardware deployment with physical sensors, which remains future work.

\textbf{Lack of cross-validation.} The results are based on a single 200/100 train/test patient split. Performance may vary across different splits, and there is a risk of optimistic or pessimistic bias. Cross-validation or multiple random splits would provide more robust estimates, particularly for the EER and BDR values that drive key agreement performance.

\textbf{NIST randomness.} The concatenated Monobit pass rate of 77.4\% indicates systematic bias in the bit sequences. While per-key tests (at 64 bits, below NIST minimum length) show higher pass rates, these are less reliable. The bias likely stems from embedding dimensions that do not produce perfectly balanced quantized outputs across the test population. Adaptive quantization boundaries, recalibrated per-patient or per-session, could address this at the cost of additional protocol complexity.

\textbf{Model generalization.} The model is trained on 200 patients from PTB-XL spanning diverse ages and conditions. The 13.3\% EER and 0.943 ROC AUC suggest reasonable discriminative capability, but extreme conditions---severe arrhythmias, pacemaker-driven rhythms---may degrade embedding correlation. Fine-tuning on the coordinator node could help, though this adds complexity.

\textbf{Temporal dynamics.} Physiological signals exhibit short-term variability (beat-to-beat) and long-term drift (circadian rhythms, medications). The 99.2\% replay failure rate validates the time-windowed refresh mechanism. The 60-second default epoch balances security against energy cost (0.345~mJ per agreement).

\textbf{Regulatory considerations.} BANs in healthcare must comply with HIPAA, GDPR, and FDA cybersecurity guidance. PhysioKey's absence of pre-shared secrets aligns with NIST SP~800-175B recommendations. The plug-and-play enrollment reduces human-mediated key compromise risk.

\textbf{The primary contribution.} While the effective key lengths are modest, the main contribution of this work is the TinyML feature extraction framework itself: demonstrating that a 6,320-parameter CNN, quantized to INT8 and running in 2.1~ms on a Cortex-M4, can extract physiologically meaningful embeddings that distinguish same-body from different-body signal pairs. This capability is a building block for physiological key agreement; improving the key length trade-off is a matter of engineering better sensors and multi-modal fusion, not a fundamental architectural limitation.

% VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced PhysioKey, an Edge-AI-driven physiological key agreement framework for Body Area Networks. A lightweight 1D-CNN with batch normalization, quantized to INT8 and occupying 31.2~KB of flash, extracts correlated embeddings from ECG signals at different body locations. Multi-level Gray code quantization and fuzzy commitment with BCH error correction enable two sensors to derive shared session keys without pre-shared secrets or trusted third parties.

Simulation on 500 patients from PTB-XL demonstrates intra-body vs.\ inter-body separability (cosine similarity 0.703 vs.\ 0.241, ROC AUC $= 0.943$) with a fundamental trade-off: BCH(63,16,23) achieves 73.2\% key agreement success with 16 effective key bits, while BCH(63,7,31) reaches 93.6\% with 7 effective bits. Total energy is 0.345~mJ per agreement---roughly $20\times$ below ECDH.

The effective key lengths are honest: 7--24 bits for the 1-bit configuration. SHA-256 provides computational stretching, but the information-theoretic security is bounded by these values. Reducing the BDR through co-located ECG-PPG sensors is the clearest path to improvement.

Future work will pursue: (1)~hardware deployment on Nordic nRF52840 and STM32L4 platforms with physical ECG and PPG sensors, which should yield lower BDR and thus stronger keys; (2)~multi-signal fusion incorporating PPG, EEG, and accelerometer data to increase embedding entropy; (3)~federated model updates that adapt $f_\theta$ across patients without centralizing data; and (4)~multi-round protocols that combine PhysioKey with a secondary authentication factor to compensate for the modest per-round key entropy.
